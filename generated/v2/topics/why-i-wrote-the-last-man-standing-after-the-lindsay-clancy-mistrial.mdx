# Why I Wrote The Last Man Standing After the Lindsay Clancy Mistrial

Jason Ansell explains the research, courtroom record and unresolved questions behind The Last Man Standing, a nonfiction examination of the Lindsay Clancy trial and mistrial.

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">On September 4, 2026, the criminal trial of Lindsay Clancy ended without a verdict.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">After weeks of testimony and nearly 40 hours of deliberations, the jury remained unable to reach a unanimous decision. Judge William Sullivan declared a mistrial, leaving the central legal question unresolved: Was Clancy criminally responsible for the deaths of her three children, or was she experiencing a mental illness so severe that she lacked criminal responsibility under Massachusetts law?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">According to reporting following the trial, the jury was divided 11–1. That outcome did not establish guilt or innocence. It meant only that the jurors could not reach the unanimous verdict required by law.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The mistrial was not the end of the case. It created another chapter—and another set of questions.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That is why I wrote <em>The Last Man Standing: Lindsay Clancy, Postpartum Psychosis, and the Juror at the Center of a Deadlock</em>.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">A case that could not be reduced to a headline</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Public discussion of the Lindsay Clancy case has often been divided into opposing narratives.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">One portrays the deaths as calculated acts for which Clancy must be held criminally responsible. The other focuses on postpartum psychosis, psychiatric treatment and the possibility that severe mental illness fundamentally altered her capacity to understand or control her actions.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The prosecution and defence presented sharply different interpretations of the same person, the same timeline and much of the same evidence.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The trial required jurors to do something far more difficult than determine whether the acts occurred. They had to decide what was happening inside Clancy’s mind at the time—and whether it satisfied Massachusetts’ legal standard for criminal responsibility.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That distinction is essential. It is also easily lost when a complicated trial is compressed into short news segments, social-media posts or emotionally charged commentary.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">Examining the evidence without pretending the questions are settled</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>The Last Man Standing</em> examines the case through the available court record, trial testimony, psychiatric evidence, treatment history and arguments presented by both sides.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The book explores:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>The events leading to the deaths of Cora, Dawson and Callan Clancy.</li>



<li>The medications, psychiatric consultations and reported changes in Lindsay Clancy’s mental condition.</li>



<li>The prosecution’s argument that her conduct demonstrated planning and awareness.</li>



<li>The defence position that she was suffering from postpartum psychosis and lacked criminal responsibility.</li>



<li>The conflicting expert interpretations presented to the jury.</li>



<li>The legal standard the jurors were required to apply.</li>



<li>The deliberations that ended in an 11–1 deadlock and a mistrial.</li>



<li>The unresolved questions now facing the court, prosecutors and defence counsel.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The purpose is not to manufacture certainty where the justice system itself did not reach it. It is to give readers enough context to understand why the case became so difficult, why reasonable people reached profoundly different conclusions and why one juror’s position prevented a final verdict.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">What the mistrial actually means</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">A mistrial is not an acquittal, and it is not a conviction.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Lindsay Clancy remains charged, and the prosecution retains the ability to try the case again. Prosecutors could seek another trial, consider a negotiated resolution or take another course permitted by law.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Judge Sullivan scheduled a hearing for September 29, 2026. That proceeding may provide greater clarity about the prosecution’s intentions and the defence’s anticipated motions. Until an official decision is made, claims that a second trial is certain—or that the case is effectively over—go beyond the confirmed record.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The book reflects the status of the proceedings following the September 4 mistrial. Any subsequent legal developments should be evaluated separately and incorporated into future case updates where appropriate.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">The importance of separating evidence from speculation</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The case has generated intense public reaction, including unsupported theories and personal allegations involving people who were not charged with any crime.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">That kind of speculation does not help readers understand the evidence. It risks turning a profound family tragedy into entertainment and can cause further harm to living individuals.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">My approach is therefore deliberately evidence-focused. The book distinguishes among:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Facts established in the public record.</li>



<li>Allegations and arguments advanced by the prosecution.</li>



<li>Evidence and interpretations presented by the defence.</li>



<li>Expert opinions that were disputed at trial.</li>



<li>Questions that remain legally unresolved.</li>
</ul>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Readers may still reach different conclusions. The goal is to ensure those conclusions are informed by the record rather than by viral claims or incomplete summaries.</p>



<h2 class="wp-block-heading">A trial about more than one legal question</h2>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The proceedings also brought renewed attention to postpartum mental illness, psychiatric prescribing practices and the difficulty families can face when seeking urgent mental-health care.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Those issues do not determine criminal responsibility by themselves. They nevertheless formed an important part of the evidence and the broader public discussion surrounding the trial.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The case asks uncomfortable questions about where medical responsibility ends and criminal responsibility begins. It also demonstrates how difficult it can be for a jury to apply legal definitions to a psychiatric condition that experts themselves interpret differently.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">No single headline can adequately address those questions.</p>

<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Because the criminal proceeding remains unresolved, I will continue monitoring official court developments. Material changes occurring after the book’s reporting cutoff will be clearly identified as updates rather than silently treated as part of the original record.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The jury did not deliver a final answer. <em>The Last Man Standing</em> examines why—and what that unresolved decision means for one of the most closely watched criminal and mental-health trials in recent years.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><em>This article and the book discuss an ongoing legal proceeding. Lindsay Clancy has pleaded not guilty, and no final determination of criminal responsibility has been reached. Nothing in this article should be interpreted as a legal or medical conclusion.</em></p>

## Key takeaways

- The Lindsay Clancy trial ended in an 11–1 deadlock, resulting in a mistrial rather than a verdict.
- The case revolves around the legal distinction between criminal responsibility and the impact of postpartum psychosis.
- A mistrial is not an acquittal, leaving the legal future of the case currently unresolved.
- Author Jason Ansell provides an evidence-based examination of the trial records and conflicting psychiatric interpretations.
