# Canada’s Bank Stablecoin Proposal Separates Brand From Liability

Canada’s proposed bank stablecoin firewall could clarify who owes the holder—but a bank logo would not by itself make the token a deposit or guarantee.

<p><strong>Analysis.</strong> Canada’s latest stablecoin proposal draws a useful line between a bank’s brand and the legal entity that owes a token holder money. That separation can make the market easier to supervise, but it also creates a disclosure test: customers must be able to see which subsidiary issued the stablecoin, what backs it and whether the parent bank has promised support.</p>
<h2>What changed on November 5</h2>
<p>Canada’s <a href="https://www.budget.canada.ca/2026/report-rapport/chap1-en.html">Budget 2026</a>, released November 5, announced an intention to require federally regulated financial institutions to issue stablecoins through non-financial institution subsidiaries regulated under the Stablecoin Act. The accompanying <a href="https://www.budget.canada.ca/2026/report-rapport/anx3-en.html">tax and legislative measures annex</a> is more explicit: the government proposes to amend the Bank Act, Trust and Loan Companies Act and Insurance Companies Act to prohibit those institutions from issuing stablecoins directly.</p>
<p>This is a proposal, not a completed amendment. Canada enacted the Stablecoin Act in March 2026, and proposed Stablecoin Regulations were published in the Canada Gazette on October 24. Those regulations are intended to come into force alongside the Act in fall 2027. The budget’s bank-subsidiary rule would require further legislation.</p>
<p>The distinction matters. Canada is not simply allowing banks to place existing deposit products on a blockchain. It is proposing that a separate legal issuer enter the same stablecoin framework as other covered non-financial issuers.</p>
<h2>The firewall clarifies the counterparty</h2>
<p>Under the proposed framework, the Bank of Canada would register and supervise non-financial stablecoin issuers. Its stated focus includes full backing by high-quality liquid assets, redemption at par, governance, risk management and enforcement. Those protections attach to the regulated issuer and its reserve arrangements.</p>
<p>A subsidiary structure can reduce ambiguity inside the regulatory system. It keeps the token issuer, reserve assets and redemption obligation inside a dedicated perimeter instead of blending them with a bank’s deposit-taking balance sheet. It also places bank-affiliated and independent issuers under a more consistent rule set.</p>
<p>But structural separation is not the same as a parent guarantee. A customer could see a familiar bank name in an app and reasonably assume the stablecoin carries the same protection as a deposit. Unless the final product documents say otherwise, the direct obligation would belong to the issuing subsidiary. The budget separately proposes higher deposit-insurance limits, but it does not say that a stablecoin issued by a non-financial subsidiary becomes an insured deposit.</p>
<h2>What builders and businesses should do</h2>
<p>Teams considering a Canadian stablecoin product should treat the issuer’s identity as a product requirement, not a disclosure buried in legal terms.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Name the legal issuer at every critical step.</strong> The wallet, checkout flow, redemption screen and API documentation should identify the entity that owes redemption. A group logo can provide context, but it should not obscure the counterparty.</li>
<li><strong>Separate reserve evidence from parent reputation.</strong> Publish the composition, custody and valuation rules for the statutory reserve, along with independent assurance where available. “Backed by a bank” is not a substitute for evidence about the issuer’s assets.</li>
<li><strong>Explain the support boundary.</strong> If the parent provides a guarantee, liquidity facility or other contractual support, describe its scope and limits. If it does not, say so plainly. Intercompany technology and treasury services should not be presented as a promise to redeem.</li>
<li><strong>Design for failure, not only normal payments.</strong> The government says it will keep consulting on consumer protection if an issuer or custodian becomes insolvent. Product teams should model a reserve custodian failure, a frozen redemption channel and a parent company choosing not to rescue the subsidiary.</li>
<li><strong>Map the adjacent regimes.</strong> The stablecoin issuer is only one layer. Wallet custody, exchange activity, payment-service functions, privacy, anti-money-laundering controls and securities questions may create separate obligations. The earlier analysis of <a href="https://jasonansell.ca/article/canada-stablecoin-framework-three-compliance-layers">Canada’s three stablecoin compliance layers</a> remains relevant.</li>
</ol>
<h2>A better test for users and procurement teams</h2>
<p>Buyers should ask four questions before treating a bank-affiliated stablecoin as cash-like infrastructure: Who is the issuer? Where are the reserve assets held? What is the contractual redemption route? What happens if the issuer or custodian enters insolvency?</p>
<p>Those questions are more useful than the logo on the wallet. They also fit a broader <a href="https://jasonansell.ca/article/tokenized-money-convertibility-test">convertibility test for tokenized money</a>: a token is only as useful as the legal, operational and liquidity path that turns it back into the unit of account.</p>
<p>For banks, the subsidiary model may preserve a route into programmable payments without placing issuance directly inside the regulated deposit-taking entity. For independent issuers, common supervision could reduce the advantage of bank branding—but only if reserve and redemption disclosures are comparable enough for customers to evaluate.</p>
<h2>Limits and what would change this assessment</h2>
<p>The bank-subsidiary requirement is an announced legislative intention. The exact amendments, transition rules, accounting treatment and supervisory coordination are not yet final. The Stablecoin Regulations are also still proposed, and the government is still considering insolvency protections. This analysis therefore describes the direction of the framework, not a product that a Canadian bank can launch today under settled rules.</p>
<p>The assessment would change if final legislation makes the parent bank directly liable, explicitly extends deposit insurance to the token, creates a statutory priority for holders, or allows direct bank issuance under prudential rules. Clear evidence that customers consistently understand subsidiary risk would also reduce the disclosure concern. Until then, the safest design principle is simple: make the legal promise as visible as the brand.</p>

## Key takeaways

- Canada proposes that banks issue stablecoins only through non-financial subsidiaries to ensure regulatory separation.
- A bank brand does not guarantee the safety of a stablecoin or equate it to a bank deposit.
- Issuers must explicitly name the legal entity responsible for redemption at all stages of the user journey.
- Market participants should prioritize evaluating reserve assets and legal obligations over brand affiliation.
