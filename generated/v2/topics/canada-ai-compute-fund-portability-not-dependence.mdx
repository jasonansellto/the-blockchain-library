# Canada’s AI Compute Fund Should Buy Portability, Not Dependence

Canada’s expanded AI Compute Access Fund can lower a real barrier for SMEs, but its strongest measure of success will be whether funded products survive after subsidies end.

<p><strong>Opinion:</strong> Canada is right to treat access to AI compute as industrial infrastructure. But a public subsidy should not merely help companies rent more processing power for a few years. It should leave them with a product that can survive the day the subsidy ends. My thesis is simple: the AI Compute Access Fund should measure portability, post-subsidy unit economics and evidence of customer value alongside dollars spent on Canadian compute.</p>

<p>On November 26, Innovation, Science and Economic Development Canada announced a $700-million expansion of the <a href="https://www.canada.ca/en/innovation-science-economic-development/news/2026/11/government-of-canada-boosts-the-ai-compute-access-fund-with-700-million-expansion.html" rel="noopener noreferrer">AI Compute Access Fund</a>. Added to the original $300 million, the program now represents $1 billion. The first new call targets health and life sciences, and the government says the expansion is intended to lower a major barrier for Canadian small and medium-sized companies: the cost of training, testing, deploying and operating AI systems.</p>

<p>That is a real constraint. Compute bills can prevent a technically credible product from reaching a customer. Yet cheaper compute does not automatically create a durable business. It can also delay a hard question: does the product still work economically when the reimbursement disappears?</p>

<h2>The subsidy is large enough to shape architecture</h2>

<p>The <a href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/ised/en/canadian-sovereign-ai-compute-strategy/ai-compute-access-fund/program-guide-ai-compute-access-fund" rel="noopener noreferrer">program guide updated November 26</a> says eligible projects can request support for compute costs between $100,000 and $5 million and run as late as March 31, 2030. It offers reimbursement of up to 80% for qualifying sovereign Canadian providers and up to 50% for other Canadian-based providers. Eligible spending includes core compute, storage, AI processing, monitoring, security and certain migration costs.</p>

<p>Those percentages are not neutral. They will influence where companies deploy, which managed services they adopt and how quickly proprietary features become embedded in their products. Encouraging Canadian-owned and Canadian-governed infrastructure is a legitimate policy choice. The risk is that a company optimizes its system for the reimbursement schedule rather than for the product's long-term cost, resilience and ability to move.</p>

<p>A strong application therefore needs more than a provider quote and a plan to consume capacity. The government's <a href="https://ised-isde.canada.ca/site/ised/en/canadian-sovereign-ai-compute-strategy/ai-compute-access-fund/application-guide-ai-compute-access-fund" rel="noopener noreferrer">application guide</a> already asks for objectives, key results, budgets, milestones, commercialization evidence, customer demand, risks and a compute-services agreement. That is a sound foundation. The next step is to make exit readiness an explicit result.</p>

<h2>Four outcomes worth measuring</h2>

<h3>1. Cost per useful outcome</h3>

<p>Teams should report the compute cost of a business result, not only total credits used. Depending on the product, that might be cost per reviewed case, completed workflow, verified document, qualified support resolution or successful customer task. The measure should include inference, retrieval, storage, monitoring, retries and human review. A falling cost per useful outcome is stronger evidence than a growing GPU bill.</p>

<h3>2. Performance after the subsidy</h3>

<p>Every funded project should model what pricing looks like at the normal commercial rate. If an 80% reimbursement is the only reason a product can serve customers, the program may be financing a temporary demonstration rather than commercialization. A credible plan should show the volume, pricing, model efficiency or product mix needed to operate without continuing support.</p>

<h3>3. Portability and an exit test</h3>

<p>Canada's own Cyber Centre advises cloud customers to avoid contracts that create lock-in and to secure access to their data at a reasonable cost. Its <a href="https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/recommended-cyber-security-contract-clauses-cloud-services-itsm50104" rel="noopener noreferrer">cloud-contract guidance</a> recommends addressing data extraction, configurations, logs, migration timing and egress charges before a move becomes urgent.</p>

<p>For AI products, portability also includes model weights where applicable, prompts, evaluation sets, vector indexes, observability data, fine-tuning artifacts and the code that connects them. This does not mean every workload must be multi-cloud. It means the company should know what is portable, what is proprietary, how long a move would take and which functions would degrade.</p>

<h3>4. Canadian capacity that remains useful</h3>

<p>The program's sovereignty goal should be evaluated by more than the address of a data centre. Useful capacity includes Canadian operations, decision-making, security expertise, support, reliable service levels and an ecosystem that can compete after the grants are spent. A subsidized workload that vanishes when funding ends creates less public value than one that produces repeat customers, reusable infrastructure and skilled jobs.</p>

<h2>What builders should do now</h2>

<p>Applicants should treat the compute agreement as part of product architecture. Before applying, build a baseline showing current workload cost, latency, reliability and customer value. Separate portable components from provider-specific ones. Document data location, subprocessors, export formats, egress fees and deletion evidence. My earlier analysis, <a href="https://jasonansell.ca/article/canada-ai-vendor-privacy-guidance-architecture-checklist">Canada's New Privacy Guidance Turns AI Vendor Selection into an Architecture Decision</a>, explains why those vendor questions also matter for privacy and accountability.</p>

<p>Then define a post-subsidy threshold: the cost and performance at which the product remains viable without reimbursement. That threshold may expose an uncomfortable answer, but discovering it before a four-year architecture hardens is exactly the kind of discipline public funding should encourage.</p>

<h2>Limits and what would change my view</h2>

<p>The public guides do not reveal every condition that ISED may place in individual contribution agreements. Project-specific reporting may already include stronger portability, unit-economics or exit requirements than the published material shows. The current health and life sciences call may also justify higher infrastructure continuity and data-residency requirements than a general software project.</p>

<p>I would revise this assessment if contribution agreements consistently require tested exit plans, post-subsidy operating models and public reporting that distinguishes temporary compute consumption from durable commercialization. Evidence that subsidized projects retain customers, reduce unit costs and continue on Canadian infrastructure after support ends would be the strongest answer.</p>

<p>Canada should fund compute access. It should also insist that access produces independence. The best result is not a company that can afford more compute while the program pays. It is a company that knows how to deliver more value with less dependence after the program stops.</p>

## Key takeaways

- Canada has expanded its AI Compute Access Fund to $1 billion for SME infrastructure support.
- Subsidies should prioritize long-term business viability over temporary consumption of compute power.
- Companies must prioritize portability to avoid vendor lock-in and prepare for post-subsidy operations.
- Measuring the cost per useful business outcome provides better evidence of value than raw compute usage.
