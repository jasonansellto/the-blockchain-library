# Self-Sovereignty

Self-sovereignty in the context of digital identity refers to the principle that individuals should have complete ownership, control, and portability over their own identity credentials, data, and digital assets. Unlike traditional identity management systems where centralized authorities issue and hold user information, self-sovereign identity (SSI) leverages decentralized ledger technology, cryptographic proofs, and decentralized identifiers (DIDs) to allow users to generate, manage, and present their verifiable credentials without relying on third-party intermediaries to act as the ultimate arbiter of their identity.

## Definition

Self-sovereignty in the context of digital identity refers to the principle that individuals should have complete ownership, control, and portability over their own identity credentials, data, and digital assets. Unlike traditional identity management systems where centralized authorities issue and hold user information, self-sovereign identity (SSI) leverages decentralized ledger technology, cryptographic proofs, and decentralized identifiers (DIDs) to allow users to generate, manage, and present their verifiable credentials without relying on third-party intermediaries to act as the ultimate arbiter of their identity.

## Simple explanation

Imagine carrying a physical wallet that holds your ID, library card, and membership passes, but the government or a company can't reach inside and change your info or block you from using them. Self-sovereignty is like having that control over your digital life. You own your identity, and you decide exactly who gets to see your information and when, rather than letting big tech companies track and control your digital profile.

## Why it matters

Self-sovereignty shifts the power balance from centralized entities to individuals, drastically reducing the impact of data breaches and preventing identity censorship. By eliminating reliance on third-party verification, it enhances personal privacy and fosters a more secure, user-centric internet where credentials are portable and tamper-proof.

## How it works

Users interact with a decentralized identity wallet that generates and stores unique decentralized identifiers (DIDs) on a blockchain. When identity verification is needed, the user issues a Verifiable Presentation signed with their private key, which the verifier checks against the issuer’s public signature on the ledger. This process uses zero-knowledge proofs to confirm identity attributes without revealing the underlying sensitive data, ensuring the user maintains control over their personal information throughout the interaction.

## Real-world example

The Hyperledger Indy project and the W3C Verifiable Credentials standard are foundational to real-world efforts like the EU's potential Digital Identity Wallet, which aims to allow citizens to prove their identity across borders without centralized tracking.

## Advantages

- Eliminates reliance on third-party identity providers
- Enhances user privacy through selective disclosure
- Prevents data silos and centralized identity breaches
- Grants users full ownership of digital credentials

## Limitations

- Requires high level of personal responsibility for keys
- Steep learning curve for average internet users
- Standardization challenges across different global systems
- Risk of permanent identity loss if keys are forgotten

## Common misconceptions

- People think self-sovereignty means complete anonymity, but it actually focuses on ownership and verifiable truth.
- Many believe it is inherently insecure, but it removes the single point of failure found in centralized databases.
