# Supply Chain Management

Supply chain management (SCM) in the context of blockchain refers to the integration of distributed ledger technology to track the provenance, movement, and ownership of goods from raw material sourcing through manufacturing to final delivery. By creating an immutable, cryptographically verifiable record of each stage, SCM on a blockchain provides unparalleled transparency, reduces administrative friction, prevents counterfeiting, and ensures accountability across complex, multi-party global supply networks. It replaces fragmented, siloed information systems with a single version of truth accessible to all permissioned stakeholders.

## Definition

Supply chain management (SCM) in the context of blockchain refers to the integration of distributed ledger technology to track the provenance, movement, and ownership of goods from raw material sourcing through manufacturing to final delivery. By creating an immutable, cryptographically verifiable record of each stage, SCM on a blockchain provides unparalleled transparency, reduces administrative friction, prevents counterfeiting, and ensures accountability across complex, multi-party global supply networks. It replaces fragmented, siloed information systems with a single version of truth accessible to all permissioned stakeholders.

## Simple explanation

Imagine every item you buy has a digital passport that gets stamped at every stop it makes—from the farm where it started to the factory that made it, all the way to your door. In a regular system, these passports are on paper and easy to lose or fake. With blockchain, this digital passport is stored on a giant, shared computer network that no one can secretly change. It is like having a perfectly honest, public diary for every single product so you can prove it is authentic and ethically sourced.

## Why it matters

Blockchain-enabled SCM solves the persistent problem of information asymmetry and lack of trust between independent entities in a global network. It is crucial for verifying authenticity in high-value industries, ensuring ethical labor standards, and optimizing logistical efficiency by providing real-time, tamper-proof data visibility.

## How it works

Participants in the supply chain record events on a distributed ledger by minting tokens or updating smart contracts that represent the physical status of goods. Each transition—such as shipping, manufacturing, or quality inspection—requires cryptographic signatures from the relevant parties, creating an audit trail that cannot be retroactively altered. Smart contracts can further automate actions like payments or releases of cargo once predefined logistical conditions are met and verified by the network.

## Real-world example

IBM Food Trust is a prominent example, used by companies like Walmart to track food provenance, allowing retailers to pinpoint the exact origin of contaminated produce in seconds rather than days to prevent widespread foodborne illness outbreaks.

## Advantages

- Immutable record of product provenance
- Enhanced transparency for end consumers
- Reduction in counterfeit goods
- Automated administrative settlement

## Limitations

- High initial implementation costs
- Dependency on accurate data entry
- Integration challenges with legacy software
- Physical-to-digital data verification risks

## Common misconceptions

- Many believe that blockchain automatically verifies physical goods, but it only tracks the digital record associated with them.
- People often assume that blockchain SCM eliminates the need for human oversight, yet physical audits remain critical for real-world validation.
