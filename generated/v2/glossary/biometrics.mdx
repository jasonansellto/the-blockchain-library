# Biometrics

Biometrics refers to the identification and verification of an individual based on their unique physiological or behavioral characteristics, such as fingerprints, facial structure, iris patterns, or gait. In a digital and cryptographic context, these biological markers are transformed into mathematical representations or hashes to authenticate identity without relying on traditional knowledge-based credentials like passwords. When integrated with blockchain technology, biometrics provide a mechanism for multi-factor authentication, ensuring that the private key controlling a digital asset remains accessible only to the authorized physical owner.

## Definition

Biometrics refers to the identification and verification of an individual based on their unique physiological or behavioral characteristics, such as fingerprints, facial structure, iris patterns, or gait. In a digital and cryptographic context, these biological markers are transformed into mathematical representations or hashes to authenticate identity without relying on traditional knowledge-based credentials like passwords. When integrated with blockchain technology, biometrics provide a mechanism for multi-factor authentication, ensuring that the private key controlling a digital asset remains accessible only to the authorized physical owner.

## Simple explanation

Think of biometrics as your body acting as your password. Instead of remembering a long, complex code that you might lose or someone might guess, you use parts of yourself—like your face or thumbprint—to prove who you are. It is like having a digital lock that only opens when it recognizes the unique pattern of your fingerprint or face, making it much harder for someone else to pretend to be you.

## Why it matters

Biometrics solve the recovery and security issues common with seed phrases by tying blockchain wallets to biological identity. They significantly enhance user experience in Web3 by simplifying authentication and reducing the risk of unauthorized access due to forgotten passwords.

## How it works

Biometric hardware scans a physical feature and processes the data through an algorithm to create a mathematical template. This template is then compared against a stored reference or used to derive cryptographic keys within a secure enclave on a device. The blockchain protocol verifies the cryptographic signature generated by this process, confirming identity without ever storing the raw biological image on-chain.

## Real-world example

The Worldcoin project uses a device called the Orb to capture high-resolution images of a person's iris, generating a unique IrisCode to verify personhood for their decentralized identity protocol.

## Advantages

- Increased resistance to unauthorized credential theft
- Eliminates reliance on easily forgotten passwords
- Seamless and fast user authentication experience

## Limitations

- Biological data cannot be reset if compromised
- Risk of false positives or sensor spoofing
- Privacy concerns regarding sensitive biometric data storage

## Common misconceptions

- People often believe the raw photo of their fingerprint is stored on the blockchain, but only a mathematical hash is used for verification.
- Many assume biometrics can replace private keys entirely, but they are typically used to unlock the device where the key resides.
