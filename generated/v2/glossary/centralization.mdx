# Centralization

Centralization is an organizational structure where decision-making power, control, and data are concentrated within a single entity or a small group of stakeholders. In a centralized system, a central authority manages network protocols, updates, and user access, often leading to increased efficiency but creating a single point of failure. This contrasts with decentralized systems, where power is distributed across a large, non-hierarchical network of participants. In the context of blockchain, centralization usually occurs in governance models or infrastructure hosting, impacting trustlessness and censorship resistance.

## Definition

Centralization is an organizational structure where decision-making power, control, and data are concentrated within a single entity or a small group of stakeholders. In a centralized system, a central authority manages network protocols, updates, and user access, often leading to increased efficiency but creating a single point of failure. This contrasts with decentralized systems, where power is distributed across a large, non-hierarchical network of participants. In the context of blockchain, centralization usually occurs in governance models or infrastructure hosting, impacting trustlessness and censorship resistance.

## Simple explanation

Think of a school club. If the president makes all the rules, manages the budget, and decides who can join, that is a centralized club. If something goes wrong with the president, the whole club might fall apart. A decentralized club would be one where every member gets a vote on every decision. Centralization is efficient for making quick changes, but it relies entirely on trusting the people in charge.

## Why it matters

The tension between centralization and decentralization is at the core of the blockchain value proposition. Excessive centralization can lead to censorship and loss of user autonomy, while moderate centralization is often used to optimize speed and scalability in early-stage projects.

## How it works

In centralized systems, a central server or entity maintains the ledger and authorizes state changes. Governance is typically managed by a board of directors or an executive team who dictate software upgrades and protocol parameters. Users must trust the central authority to act in their best interest and maintain the security of the infrastructure.

## Real-world example

The Tether (USDT) stablecoin is considered centralized because a single company, Tether Limited, manages the issuance, reserves, and redemption of the tokens.

## Advantages

- Faster decision-making processes
- Easier to deploy updates
- Greater system-level efficiency

## Limitations

- Single point of failure
- Susceptible to censorship
- Requires high user trust

## Common misconceptions

- Some think centralization is always 'bad' in blockchain, though it can offer necessary speed for enterprise use cases. Many assume that having a CEO means a project is fully centralized, ignoring that some projects have decentralized governance despite centralized leadership.
