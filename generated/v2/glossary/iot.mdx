# IoT

The Internet of Things (IoT) refers to the vast network of physical objects—'things'—embedded with sensors, software, and connectivity technologies that enable them to collect and exchange data across the internet. In the context of blockchain, IoT serves as a distributed data layer, providing verifiable, immutable, and time-stamped information from the physical world. By integrating IoT with decentralized ledgers, stakeholders can achieve autonomous machine-to-machine transactions, automated supply chain tracking, and improved security, effectively bridging the gap between digital distributed systems and physical infrastructure.

## Definition

The Internet of Things (IoT) refers to the vast network of physical objects—'things'—embedded with sensors, software, and connectivity technologies that enable them to collect and exchange data across the internet. In the context of blockchain, IoT serves as a distributed data layer, providing verifiable, immutable, and time-stamped information from the physical world. By integrating IoT with decentralized ledgers, stakeholders can achieve autonomous machine-to-machine transactions, automated supply chain tracking, and improved security, effectively bridging the gap between digital distributed systems and physical infrastructure.

## Simple explanation

Imagine your refrigerator, your car, and your doorbell all talking to each other and the internet without you needing to press any buttons. That is the Internet of Things. When we add blockchain, these gadgets don't just talk; they keep a digital record of everything they do that no one can fake. It is like giving every smart device a secure, personal diary that everyone in the network can trust, allowing them to trade or share information safely on their own.

## Why it matters

IoT allows blockchain technology to interact directly with the physical world, creating a bridge for real-time automation and verifiable data. It is essential for eliminating intermediaries in supply chains and enabling machine-to-machine economies that do not require human intervention.

## How it works

IoT devices are equipped with sensors that capture environmental or operational data and transmit it to a blockchain via an API or middleware. Once the data reaches the ledger, smart contracts automatically trigger actions, such as releasing payments upon delivery or updating inventory status. The blockchain acts as a decentralized registry, ensuring that the sensor data remains tamper-proof and cryptographically verified throughout the device's lifecycle.

## Real-world example

The Helium Network, which utilizes a decentralized infrastructure of LoRaWAN hotspots to provide wireless connectivity for IoT devices, rewarding participants with HNT tokens for verifying network coverage.

## Advantages

- Automated, trustless machine-to-machine interactions
- Enhanced supply chain transparency and tracking
- Immutable data integrity for sensor readings

## Limitations

- Vulnerability to faulty or spoofed sensor data
- Significant hardware resource and power constraints
- High latency in data synchronization across networks

## Common misconceptions

- People often think IoT refers only to consumer smart home devices, but it primarily concerns industrial and infrastructure monitoring.
- Some believe blockchain is required for all IoT, when it is actually only necessary for decentralized verification and automated settlement.
