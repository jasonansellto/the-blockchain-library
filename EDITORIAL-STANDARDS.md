# Editorial Standards

## Purpose

The Blockchain Library exists to educate. Every contribution should prioritize clarity, technical accuracy, neutrality, evidence quality, and long-term usefulness.

## Voice and tone

Preferred tone:

- professional;
- calm;
- explanatory;
- precise;
- neutral.

Avoid:

- hype;
- tribal language;
- sensational claims;
- investment recommendations;
- certainty unsupported by evidence;
- unnecessary jargon.

## Audience progression

Content should support multiple levels.

### Beginner

Answer:

- What is it?
- Why does it exist?
- Why does it matter?
- What should I understand next?

### Intermediate

Add:

- architecture;
- trade-offs;
- dependencies;
- common failure modes;
- security implications.

### Advanced

Add:

- implementation assumptions;
- protocol mechanics;
- operational considerations;
- comparisons;
- standards and research.

## Accuracy

Do not invent missing facts.

If a claim is uncertain, emerging, disputed, or dependent on implementation, say so.

When a concept varies across protocols, distinguish the general concept from protocol-specific behavior.

## Neutrality

The Blockchain Library does not endorse tokens, investment strategies, political positions, or companies.

Vendor or project examples may be used when technically helpful, but promotional claims should not be copied into neutral educational material without qualification.

## Evidence hierarchy

Prefer, in approximate order:

1. official technical specifications and standards;
2. official documentation;
3. peer-reviewed or reputable academic research;
4. protocol white papers and technical papers;
5. primary-source engineering material;
6. high-quality educational references.

Social media, anonymous posts, and promotional pages should not be the sole support for important technical claims.

## Citations

When references materially support a claim, include enough metadata to identify the source.

For research, include DOI or other persistent identifiers when verified.

Do not invent bibliographic details.

## Terminology

Use canonical terminology consistently.

When multiple terms exist:

- choose one preferred term;
- list common aliases where useful;
- avoid silently switching terms for stylistic variety when that reduces precision.

## Topic structure

A mature topic should generally be able to support:

1. overview;
2. definition;
3. why it matters;
4. how it works;
5. examples;
6. risks and limitations;
7. related topics;
8. developer perspective;
9. books and research;
10. references.

Not every topic requires all sections in one page. The Knowledge Engine may distribute them across related objects.

## Glossary standards

Glossary definitions should be concise, neutral, and self-contained.

A glossary entry should not become a full tutorial or opinion piece.

## Security content

Never publish:

- private keys;
- seed phrases;
- passwords;
- production credentials;
- active secrets.

Examples must use obvious placeholders.

Security guidance should emphasize safe behavior and avoid normalizing risky operational practices.

## Code examples

Code should be:

- minimal;
- readable;
- relevant to one concept;
- clear about assumptions.

Include warnings where examples omit production concerns such as error handling, validation, permissions, authentication, or network checks.

## AI-assisted writing

AI may assist drafting, restructuring, summarization, classification, metadata creation, and consistency checking.

AI output does not bypass editorial review.

Before publication, verify:

- factual claims;
- terminology;
- citations;
- relationships;
- code behavior;
- security implications.

## Research content

Research objects should preserve the title, author, identifiers, and scope of the source.

Do not silently broaden a paper's claims beyond what the paper supports.

## Accessibility

Prefer short paragraphs, descriptive headings, meaningful link text, readable diagrams, and explanations of specialized terms.

Avoid relying on color alone to communicate meaning.

## Updates

Content should be reviewed when:

- protocol behavior changes;
- standards evolve;
- links break;
- referenced software is deprecated;
- a definition becomes misleading;
- stronger primary evidence becomes available.

## Review checklist

Before publication, verify:

- [ ] technically accurate;
- [ ] neutral tone;
- [ ] terminology consistent;
- [ ] sources appropriate;
- [ ] internal links valid;
- [ ] relationships valid;
- [ ] metadata complete;
- [ ] security review considered;
- [ ] code examples scoped correctly;
- [ ] beginner readability appropriate where intended;
- [ ] no secrets or private information included.

## Editorial principle

The goal is not to publish more content.

The goal is to improve understanding while strengthening the consistency of the knowledge graph.
